The 56% that was credited to the wrong channel

Two museums, one Salesforce org, and a marketing budget being allocated on channel numbers that had never been audited. I rebuilt multi-touch attribution across $3.5M of membership, ticketing, and donation revenue, and then did the part that actually mattered: made the number stay right without anyone maintaining it.

Context
Year-long analytics practicum at a two-site museum institution with no dedicated data or IT support
My role
Sole analyst. Scoped it, built it, and presented it to executive stakeholders
Stack
Salesforce, Snowflake, SQL, Python, Tableau, Gemini API
Headline
$3.5M reattributed, 56% legacy misattribution corrected, executive reporting from 15 hours a week to 2

A museum's revenue does not arrive the way a CRM expects it to. Someone buys a ticket for an exhibition they saw on a bus shelter, comes back three months later on a friend's recommendation, converts to a membership at the admissions desk, and becomes a donor two years after that. Standard campaign attribution logic sees a fraction of that. It fills in the rest with whatever touch happens to be attached to the record.

The problem I was handed, and the one I found

I was brought in to build executive reporting. The institution had never had an analytics function, so reporting meant someone pulling numbers by hand each week: roughly 15 hours of assembly before anyone could look at a trend. That was the stated problem.

The real problem showed up while I was validating the source data. Channel performance, the number that decided where marketing spend went, did not survive scrutiny. A large share of real-world acquisition arrived through paths that bypassed the standard CRM campaign logic entirely, and those conversions were not going uncredited. They were being credited to whichever channel the logic could see.

So the institution was not flying blind. It was flying on instruments that were confidently pointing the wrong way, which is worse, because nobody had a reason to doubt them.

What I built

Fixing the history and fixing the future are two different jobs, and the second one is the one that lasts. I did both, in this order.

  1. The museum's first ELT pipeline No dedicated IT support, so I architected and built it: 1.2M Salesforce accounts and over 100K won opportunities ingested into Snowflake, on a schedule, with the warehouse as the place analysis actually happens instead of a stack of exports.
  2. Identity resolution in SQL Attribution is meaningless if one human being is four account records. I deduplicated the account base in SQL so that a visitor's ticket purchase, membership, and gift resolved to the same person before any model touched them.
  3. A rewritten multi-touch attribution model Rebuilt over membership, ticketing, and donation revenue together rather than as three separate funnels, because at this institution they are one relationship at different stages.
  4. An AI agent for the data rules could not reach The marketing source data that bypassed CRM logic was unstructured, which is exactly why rule-based logic had failed on it. I built an agent to resolve it, ran it in batch over Snowflake to correct the legacy record, and validated its output with a Python workflow rather than trusting it.
  5. Verification moved to the moment of capture Then I deployed the agent natively in Salesforce, where it investigates and verifies each new campaign member's true source before the conversion is recorded. This is the part I care about: the backfill was a one-time cleanup, this is why the number does not drift back.
  6. Tableau KPI dashboards on top Published on the Snowflake models so executives read the same corrected numbers I did. Weekly reporting went from roughly 15 hours of assembly to about 2 hours of maintenance.
The thing worth taking away

The misattribution was not a bug. It was standard CRM logic meeting real acquisition behavior and quietly filling the gap. Backfilling the history gets you a better slide; moving verification to the moment of capture is what keeps the slide true next quarter.

What changed once the numbers were right

Corrected attribution is only interesting if somebody spends differently because of it. A three-year purchase analysis showed the pattern the old numbers had hidden: acquisition campaigns had drifted away from lapsed members toward cold ticket buyers, which explained a one-year collapse in membership acquisition ROI that nobody had been able to account for.

We relaunched win-back campaigns against the population the data actually pointed at.

Also at FAMSF

The attribution rebuild was the spine of the year, but it was not the only thing. These were separate work streams on the same foundation, not downstream effects of the attribution fix.

  • Retention and churn modeling. Segmented every account in the CRM and built models predicting who would lapse and who would upgrade, then routed high churn-risk members into targeted renewal sequences and engaged multi-year members into donor upgrade campaigns. Member retention improved 6 points; donor churn fell 15%.
  • Checkout experimentation. Identified which channels produced members with the highest lifetime value, then A/B tested checkout messaging and pricing tiers with the marketing and membership teams across four exhibition seasons. Checkout conversion rose 18%, adding over 2,500 high-LTV members and first-time donors.
  • Ticketing as a sales funnel. Applied drop-off analysis to the B2C online checkout and built an agent that enriched inbound group sales leads with external web data to score and prioritize high-value B2B buyers. Ticket revenue grew 11% across both museum locations.
  • An A/B test tracking framework so experiment results were recorded consistently instead of living in whoever ran the test's notes.

What I would do differently written for you

I built the batch correction first and the capture-time verification second, because the backfill was what stakeholders had asked for and it was the faster win. In hindsight I would argue harder for reversing that order. Every week the verification step did not exist was another week of new records arriving with the same defect, and the batch job had to be rerun over a moving target. The cleanup is the visible deliverable; the intake control is the one that decides whether you ever have to do the cleanup again.

The other thing I underweighted: I validated the agent's output against a Python workflow, which was right, but I did not build a standing monitor on its resolution rate. If its accuracy degraded six months after I left, nothing would have raised a hand.
