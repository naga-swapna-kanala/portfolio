Account clearance from days to under four hours

A global enterprise software company was catching restricted parties at audit rather than at onboarding, and its sales teams had no way to see why an account was stuck. I built screening into the onboarding path so it happened in real time. Then I spent the next two years on the harder problem: teaching the system which alerts deserved a human.

Context
Denied-party and sanctions screening platform on Salesforce, serving the client's global sales teams
My role
Owned the screening decision logic, the Snowflake models behind it, and the reporting leadership reviewed monthly. Worked directly with Compliance and Legal
Stack
Salesforce, Snowflake, dbt, SQL, Tableau, third-party screening vendor over a MuleSoft integration layer
Headline
Clearance from days to under 4 hours, $2M+ quarterly pipeline unblocked, false positives down roughly 40%

Denied-party screening is a legal obligation that behaves, operationally, like a revenue gate. Every new account has to be checked against sanctions and restricted-party lists before business proceeds. Do it slowly and you are not just non-compliant on paper, you are sitting on deals that cannot close. Do it badly and you bury a compliance team in matches that were never real.

What was actually broken

Three things, and they compounded.

  • Screening happened after the fact. Restricted parties were surfacing during audit cycles rather than at account onboarding, which is the difference between a control and a post-mortem.
  • Sales had no visibility. An account in review looked identical to an account nobody had touched. Reps escalated by asking people, and operations answered by looking things up by hand.
  • The alert volume was mostly noise, and the noise regenerated. Re-screens resubmitted already-adjudicated alerts as new ones, so decisions a reviewer had made were overwritten and had to be made again. Gaps in vendor responses turned into manual work rather than into retries.

The third one is the one that mattered. You can make a slow process fast and still have a compliance team spending its week on matches that were never real.

What I built

  1. Real-time screening at account onboarding I built the Salesforce side of the platform, calling a third-party screening vendor through a MuleSoft integration layer maintained by the enterprise integration team, so accounts were checked as they were created rather than in a later sweep. Clearance status became a field Sales could see, which removed the escalation-by-asking loop entirely.
  2. A redesign of how matches were ranked, decided, and auto-closed Across four account types, each with genuinely different risk profiles. The two design rules that did the heavy lifting: a re-check can never overwrite a prior human decision, and a missing vendor response is a retry condition, not a manual queue item.
  3. dbt models in Snowflake for segment-specific logic Screening rules that differ by account segment belong in version-controlled transformations, not in whoever remembers the exception. I designed dbt models implementing type-specific screening logic across the four segments for both new-account and update pipelines, which eliminated manual assessment updates and cut evidence mis-tagging by 30%.
  4. Upstream data-quality controls Deduplication logic and match precision controls so re-screens stopped resubmitting adjudicated alerts as new. This removed about 30 hours a month of redundant review effort, which is the kind of saving that only exists if you fix the cause rather than triage the symptom.
  5. A Salesforce to Snowflake ELT pipeline, with results written back And a standardized account clearance lifecycle as the single source of truth for Sales, Compliance, and downstream systems, so status meant the same thing everywhere. That alone removed roughly 10 hours a week of manual report building and status lookups.
  6. SQL-backed Tableau dashboards on backlog aging and stuck records Not a vanity dashboard: the monthly business review ran off it, for Sales, Legal, Risk, and Trade Compliance leadership. I prepared and presented the materials and drove the resulting actions to completion by the following cycle.
The thing worth taking away

Speed was the metric leadership asked for. Precision was the metric that changed how the team worked. Cutting clearance to four hours unblocked deals; cutting false positives by 40% meant the hours a reviewer did spend went to matches that might actually be real.

The night the pipeline went quiet

A silent failure is the worst kind, because nothing alarms. Alerts stopped being delivered to the trade compliance teams and the system looked healthy from the outside. By the time it surfaced, more than 70,000 alerts were undelivered.

I traced it to root cause, an expired credential in the ELT path, and restored and reconciled the full backlog overnight out of the Snowflake staging layer. Then, because clearing a backlog is not the same as clearing it in the right order, I implemented three-tier prioritization to route alerts to the trade compliance teams by urgency. The backlog cleared within SLA and review turnaround came down from days to hours.

The part I would not repeat: the reason this became a 70,000-record incident rather than a 200-record one is that nothing was watching for the absence of delivery. A pipeline that monitors its own failures is table stakes; a pipeline that monitors its own silence is the thing I now build first.

Getting Legal and Risk to say yes

The hardest sign-off was the partner screening criteria, which touched enough edge cases that reviewing it as a document went nowhere for weeks. I stopped writing and built instead: two working prototypes in sandboxes, the data flows mapped, and hands-on demos where Legal and Risk Operations could try the edge cases they were worried about rather than imagine them.

Sign-off went from three weeks to four days, and the chosen design deployed to 30,000 production accounts with zero regression to the existing rules. I have used the same move since: when a written spec stalls with a risk-averse reviewer, the fastest path is usually something they can click.

What I would do differently written for you

I sequenced this wrong at the start. Real-time screening was the visible commitment, so it went first, and the alert-precision redesign followed. That meant the first months of real-time screening delivered a faster flow of largely false alerts into a team that was already underwater, and I spent credibility there that I did not need to spend. If I ran it again I would ship precision controls and real-time capture closer together, even at the cost of a later launch date.

I would also push earlier for adoption evidence. I know the clearance-status field removed the escalation loop because the escalations stopped arriving, but I never instrumented rep usage directly, and "the complaints stopped" is a weaker claim than it needs to be.
