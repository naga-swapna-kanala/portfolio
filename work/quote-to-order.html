Tata Consultancy Services • Salesforce CPQ Developer, then Sales Operations Analyst • Telecom client • Jun 2016 - Oct 2021

Excel quotes to rule-driven CPQ, and a $150M pipeline you could forecast

Five years on one quote-to-order and order-orchestration platform, in two distinct halves. First I built the rails: order automation, a reconciliation that found a million dollars nobody had billed, and the CPQ configuration behind every new product launch. Then I moved onto the rails and ran the sales operating cadence on top of them.

Context
Enterprise telecom quote-to-order platform: complex bundles, provisioning, and a separate billing system that had to agree with the CRM
My role
Built the quoting and order automation as a CPQ developer, then owned quoting, discount governance, and the forecast as a sales operations analyst
Stack
Salesforce CPQ, Apex, Flows, SQL, Snowflake, Tableau, advanced Excel
Headline
Order failures from over 50% to about 5%, $1M unbilled revenue found, quote errors down 35%, forecast variance inside 10%

Enterprise telecom quoting is where pricing logic goes to be tested. A single deal can carry a broadband circuit, a managed router, an installation charge, a promotional discount that applies to two of those three, and a contract term that changes all of it. Every one of those lines has to survive the trip from quote to order to provisioning to invoice. When it does not, nobody notices until a customer does.

Act I: building the rails, 2016 to 2018

Orders that failed more often than they succeeded

Orders were being created by hand after a deal closed, and I could not get a straight answer on how often that went wrong, so I went to the order and billing data to find out. The failure rate on manual order creation was over 50%. Half of the handoffs between a won deal and a fulfillable order were breaking, and the breakage was absorbed as rework rather than recorded as a defect.

I automated the opportunity-to-order handoff in Salesforce so orders were generated at opportunity closure, from the configured quote, rather than retyped from it. Failures fell to roughly 5%.

The million dollars that was assumed billed

The order automation raised an uncomfortable question. If the handoff had been failing half the time for years, what had happened to the orders that failed quietly? I ran a quote-to-cash reconciliation in Snowflake, using SQL to match provisioned orders in the CRM against billed orders in the billing system, on the assumption that the two populations should be identical.

They were not. About $1M of revenue had been provisioned and delivered to customers but never billed for. I walked the exceptions through Finance, Fulfillment, and Sales Operations to confirm each one was real rather than a matching artifact, then built re-push automation to send the validated orders back through to billing. $600K was recovered within 60 days.

The thing worth taking away

A reconciliation is just two systems that ought to agree, and asking why they do not. It is the least glamorous query I know how to write and it has found more money than anything else I have built.

Owning the systems side of product launches

Every new plan and bundle Marketing and Product designed had to exist in the quoting system before a rep could sell it. That was mine: product records, price book entries, bundle structures with option constraints, and price rules in Salesforce CPQ, for 30+ new plans and bundles across 15+ launches, each delivered on the launch date Marketing and Product had already committed to publicly.

The part that kept invoices correct was less visible: mapping each new SKU to its billing charge code, so a promotional bundle, discounted enterprise broadband with a managed router, say, quoted and invoiced correctly from launch day rather than being discovered as a billing defect in month two.

I also said no a fair amount. When a pricing or packaging design could not be quoted or billed as specified, I pushed back and brought a configurable alternative instead of shipping something that would break downstream. That is easier to do when you can show the mechanism rather than just object.

Act II: running the motion, 2018 to 2021

The Proposal stage where deals went to sit

Pipeline analysis kept surfacing the same shape: deals stalling at Proposal. The cause was not rep behavior. Quotes were still being assembled in legacy Excel workbooks, which meant every non-standard configuration was a manual build, and every manual build was a chance to pick the wrong product.

I investigated the pricing and SKU data first to find and remove the product-selection anomalies, because migrating bad reference data into a new system just relocates the problem. Then I worked with Sales, Product, Finance, and IT to move quoting into Salesforce CPQ with rule-driven product and pricing configuration, and trained the sales team on it.

Discount governance, so margin stopped leaking one exception at a time

Non-standard enterprise deals were approved case by case, which meant the approval bar moved depending on who was asked. I profiled the actual discount patterns in the historical data first, then used that to set approval criteria, thresholds, and escalation paths in approval matrices, so the rule reflected how the business really priced rather than what anyone believed about it. Pricing exceptions fell 20% and contract margins held.

A forecast leadership could plan against

I owned the weighted forecast model for a $150M pipeline across four regions and 60+ reps, derived from historical win rates and stage velocity rather than from rep confidence. A model alone would not have held, though, because a forecast is only as good as the pipeline data underneath it. So the model came with a weekly hygiene inspection against stage exit criteria and validation rules, flagging stale opportunities, incomplete data, and deals overdue in stage.

Quarterly forecast variance came down from 15% to within 10%.

The weekly top-deals review

I ran it for sales leadership across all four regions, and the value was in the preparation rather than the meeting. Each deal got a card showing last activity, close-date pushes, and approval status, which turns "how are we tracking" into a specific conversation about specific deals.

In one quarter that cadence surfaced $3M of at-risk commit six weeks before quarter end, while there was still time to act on it. Slippage fell 40%.

The rest of the operating layer

  • Self-serve sales performance dashboards in Salesforce and Tableau on pipeline coverage, stage conversion, win rate, and opportunity aging, built for reps and managers rather than only for leadership, so coverage gaps and conversion drops were visible between reviews instead of at them. Follow-up efficiency improved 15%.
  • Customer master data governance in Salesforce: account hierarchies of parent and child accounts, contact roles, and partner relationships, alongside account and opportunity onboarding and screening. Unglamorous, and the reason the hierarchy-dependent reporting above could be trusted.

What I would do differently written for you

The CPQ migration's weakest link was adoption, and I treated training as the answer to it. It mostly was, but reps who had built quotes in Excel for years had workarounds I only learned about months later, usually when a strange configuration appeared in the data. If I ran the migration again I would sit with the three highest-volume quoters for a day each before designing the rules, not after, because their workarounds were requirements I had not been told about.

On the forecast: I tuned it toward accuracy at the quarter level, which is what I was measured on. Leadership repeatedly wanted to ask a different question, what happens if this region slips, and my model could not answer that cleanly. I would build scenario branches in from the start rather than bolting on one-off what-if analyses each time the question came up.
